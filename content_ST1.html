Ghost Nets:
The Silent Hunters of the Deep

When nets are left behind, the ocean keeps killing long after we’re gone.

Kulith Silva · STUDENT 1
The Predators That Never Sleep

Not alive,
yet it hunts without end.

Introduction — signals of a resilient sea

Picture a hunter in the ocean who never gets tired, never needs to eat, and never grows old. This hunter is not a giant fish or a shark. It is not made of muscle and bone, nor is it made of metal; it is made of plastic and nylon. These are called ghost nets.

A net once used to fish becomes a hunter that never stops.
The Immortal Menace

Built to last forever,
designed to destroy forever.

How modern materials turned fishing nets into permanent ocean threats.

The biggest problem with these nets is that they are immortal. In the old days, fishing nets were made of natural materials such as wood, cotton, or rope. If those nets were lost, they would eventually rot and return to the environment. But today, nets are made of very strong plastic. This plastic is designed to never break.

The Deadliest Plastic

The most dangerous plastic isn’t swallowed—it traps and kills.

Ghost nets silently entangle marine life, making escape nearly impossible.

Many people say that plastic bags and straws are bad for the ocean. However, ghost nets are the most dangerous type of plastic in the ocean. The World Wildlife Fund (2025) reports that these "immortal menaces" harm over 66% of marine mammal species and all species of sea turtles. A ghost net is not just debris—it is a trap, while a plastic bag is usually swallowed by mistake. These nets have created a danger that is almost impossible to see and even harder to escape.

The Cycle of the Garbage Patch

Ghost nets don’t just trap fish
they suffocate the ocean’s homes.

From coral reefs to the Garbage Patch, these nets keep killing in a never-ending cycle.

70–86% Microplastics from nets

1+ year Coral smothered & resurfaced

These nets do more than just catch fish. They ruin the ocean's "homes". A heavy net that falls on a coral reef acts like a heavy blanket, keeping the coral from getting the sunlight it needs to live. It can also break the coral into smaller pieces. A net can get so full of dead fish that it sinks to the bottom. The net gets light again as the fish rot, and it comes back up to the surface and starts killing again. In the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, fishing-related gear makes up a staggering 70% to 86% of floating microplastics by weight (Ocean Conservancy, 2024).

Invisible, immortal, and relentless—the ocean’s silent predator.
Breaking the Invisible Web

When humans act,
the ocean can heal.

By redesigning nets and cleaning up the ocean, we can stop ghost nets from killing endlessly.

The whole world needs to work together to stop these "silent hunters". Some people are making parts of the nets out of special materials that break down or melt in saltwater after a few months. These parts will break if a trap is lost, allowing animals to swim to safety. This means that the "hunter" finally stops hunting when the fisherman leaves. We can make the ocean a safe place for animals again by cleaning up the nets and using better tools.

The ocean doesn’t forget what we leave behind—every net we abandon can keep hunting long after we’re gone.
